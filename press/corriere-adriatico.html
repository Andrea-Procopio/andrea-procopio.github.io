Andrea, from Liceo Galilei to research at Harvard: “I teach AI to think like us humans”

A profile of Andrea Procopio’s path to Harvard and his research on AI and human perception.

This is an unofficial English translation of an article first published in Italian. Read the original on Corriere Adriatico.

ANCONA — Andrea Procopio has travelled since he was a child. “I was born in Chieti, but I spent most of my time in Forlì,” he says. Then he arrived in Ancona for his final year at the science-focused Liceo Galilei, where he graduated in 2023. “That’s the year that stayed closest to my heart, the one when I did the most,” he admits.

The leap

Then his inclination to travel struck again, taking him to Bocconi University in Milan to study Economics, Management and Computer Science, taught in English. “It’s a course that has become increasingly focused on machine learning [computers learn from data and improve on their own — editor’s note], with an economics perspective but a broad approach that lets you do many different things,” explains Andrea, who was born in 2004. In his case, for example, it allowed him to conduct research into artificial intelligence at Harvard while studying for his degree at Bocconi.

We could try to understand what he is working on, but it would probably be quicker to enjoy the results of his and his colleagues’ work when it improves our daily lives. “In ten years, every person living in a developed country will interact with robots on a daily basis,” he says. “Our goal is not just to have robots that are very good at perceiving the world; we also want them to understand how we humans perceive it. This is called alignment, and we have found a way to take any AI model and make it more like humans,” he explains.

At Harvard, where he worked in person until December and will continue remotely until July, when he graduates from Bocconi, Andrea studies computer vision: the branch of artificial intelligence that allows computers to interpret the world visually, as we do. But Procopio is already thinking about the future. “Right now, I’m applying to master’s programmes here in America.” And after that? “My goal is to start my own tech company,” he says, to conduct research. All in good time, though.

Amid all this, an obvious question arises: why not in Italy? In fact, Andrea has also studied here, at Marche Polytechnic University, with Professor Paola Pierleoni. He recalls: “I worked on machine learning to create lightweight models that would allow Parkinson’s patients to be monitored at home through wearable devices.”

Reflections

Ancona, too, has excellent research, but “in many places in Italy or Europe, the work I do doesn’t even exist,” he says. “It’s a question of infrastructure; if the opportunity were there, I would stay in Italy.” And he does not rule out returning one day. There are other young Italians at Harvard with him. What he appreciates about America, however, is “the more open-minded atmosphere, the greater freedom to think and act.” Even to make mistakes. He jokes: “I’m not an alien; I’ve just always tried to do as many things as possible.” And that approach clearly works.
